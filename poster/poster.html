Quantum Deep Learning Still
Needs a Quantum Leap

1MIT FutureTech, CSAIL, Cambridge MA, USA   |   2TU Wien, Vienna, Austria

Forty-Third International Conference on Machine Learning

Read the paper

Our Position

Even accounting for rapid hardware progress, quantum computers will need a quantum leap — not just steady improvement — to meaningfully accelerate deep learning over the next 10–20 years.

Why Ask Now?

Deep learning's progress tracks compute — the “bitter lesson.” As classical hardware nears its limits, it is natural to ask whether quantum hardware could drive the next paradigm shift, as GPUs once did.

We present a first-of-its-kind survey mapping quantum algorithms onto every stage of the deep-learning pipeline, paired with quantitative economic-advantage forecasts built on real quantum hardware trends.

The verdict: today's promising speedups are blocked by overhead, missing memory, or apply only to narrow special cases.

Fig 1 · Quantum Economic Advantage (QEA). Quantum gains are asymptotic but carry a huge constant hardware overhead. A problem pays off on quantum only beyond a critical threshold N* — below it, classical is faster.

The QEA Model

We extend the Quantum Economic Advantage framework of Choi et al. (2023). Quantum devices have asymptotic advantages but enormous constants:

  • ~1013× slower gate speed vs. a floating-point op
  • ~102× overhead from quantum error correction
  • ~108× more GPU parallelism per dollar classically

We forecast how qubit counts, gate speeds, and error rates evolve, then compute when—if ever—each algorithm crosses its threshold.

Fig 2 · Quantum vs. classical search. Accounting for hardware trends and a 1-month runtime limit, Grover-type search reaches economic advantage only around 2034, and only for problem sizes far larger than real deep-learning search.

Robustness

We vary every growth rate and constant in the model across 10−3–104. The predicted advantage year is largely insensitive to these changes.

The one real lever is the quantum gate-speed improvement rate (beyond Moore's law), which materially shifts the outlook for matrix multiplication. Grover predictions are robust — these algorithms are time-bound, not qubit-bound.

Fig 3 · Advantage by algorithm class. Problem sizes in the shaded region are feasible on a quantum computer; only those above the orange QEA line benefit. (a) exponential speedups can win by ~2033; (b) Grover wins only at enormous sizes (~2034); (c) dense matrix multiplication stays out of reach.

3Three Roadblocks

1
Overhead swamps matmul. Quantum matrix multiplication beats classical O(N3) asymptotically, but constant overhead makes it infeasible at any practical size for foreseeable hardware.
2
QRAM is missing. The most powerful speedups — q-means, quantum PCA, HHL-based training, kernel methods — all depend on practical QRAM, which remains underdeveloped.
3
Special cases only. Grover-type quadratic speedups need search sizes > 1024 to pay off — far beyond real hyperparameter or RL action search.

Call to Action

  • Accelerate QRAM — it gates most of the promising exponential speedups.
  • Push quantum gate speed and cut error-correction overhead beyond current roadmaps.
  • Target problems with both a clear asymptotic advantage and viable problem sizes.
  • Stay skeptical of exponential “training” claims that rest on heavy preconditions.

Quantum's near-term role in deep learning is narrow. The opportunity is real — but realizing it requires a leap, not a step.
